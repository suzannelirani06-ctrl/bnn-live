Can a small group change a whole room?

This page is about a 1969 experiment with one simple question: if a few people keep giving the same answer, can they change what everyone else says? Here is what the researchers did, what they found, and what it might mean.

Key words

Words you will see

You don't need any psychology background. These are the only special words on the page.

Majority

The bigger group in the room.

Minority

The smaller group. In this study, it is the few people who keep giving the same answer.

Minority influence

When a small group changes what the bigger group thinks or says.

Consistent

Giving the same answer every time, instead of changing it to fit in.

Confederate

A helper who secretly works with the researchers. Everyone else thinks they are a normal participant.

Compliance

Going along with the group in public while still privately thinking the same way.

Conversion

Really changing your own mind, not just what you say out loud.

Afterimage

The colour you see when you look at a white wall after staring at a coloured picture.

What happened

The 1969 study, step by step

Before this study, most research asked how a big group pulls people into line. Solomon Asch had shown that people often went along with a group that gave an obviously wrong answer.

In 1969, Serge Moscovici flipped the question. What if the people pushing are the few, not the many? Can a small group that holds its position change the bigger group?

What happens when a small group keeps its answer?

The study had 172 women. They sat in groups of six. Four of them were real participants who did not know the setup. Two were secret helpers. Everyone looked at 36 slides that were clearly blue, though some were lighter and some darker. Each person said the colour out loud.

The room came in three versions. In the first, there were no helpers. In the second, the helpers said green on some slides and blue on others. In the third, the helpers said green on every slide.

Why does the third version matter? A helper who keeps changing their answer looks unsure. A helper who always says green looks sure of it, and that is harder for others to brush off.

Top stories

The story in four cards

THE SETUP

Six people per room, two of them secret helpers

Each room had four real participants and two helpers who had been told what to say.

Read more

The real participants did not know the two helpers were working with the researchers. The helpers gave the same answer on every slide, which is what made them a steady minority.

THE NUMBERS

From about 1 in 400 to about 1 in 12

When the helpers always said green, green answers went up a lot.

Read more

With no helpers, about 0.25% of answers were green, or about 1 in 400. With helpers who changed their answers, about 1.25%. With helpers who always said green, 8.42%, or about 1 in 12.

THE DEBATE

Did people really change their minds?

From the outside, going along and changing your mind can look the same.

Read more

Someone can say green to fit in while still seeing blue. The researchers said a steady minority can change what people privately think, not just what they say. That is the part people still argue about.

LATER TESTS

Not every later test agreed

A 1980 study by Sorrentino, King and Leo failed to replicate the result.

Read more

They found the colour shift only among participants who were highly suspicious of the study. They argued that suspicion about the study could explain the shifts.

The debate

Going along or changing your mind?

Compliance

Saying what the group says to fit in, while still thinking your own way. Example: agreeing with your friends at dinner to avoid an argument, even though you disagree.

Conversion

Really changing your own view. Example: you hear the same argument from the same person, again and again, until you start to see it differently.

Moscovici argued that when a minority stays steady, people stop and think about its argument. Thinking it through is what can lead to a real change of mind, not just going along.

Did it hold up? Not every test agreed. In 1980, Sorrentino, King and Leo failed to replicate the result, and their extra data pointed to suspicion about the study. The debate is not settled, and this page doesn't claim it is.

Questions answered

Your questions, answered simply

What is minority influence?

It is when a small group changes what a bigger group thinks or says. This study tested whether a small group that keeps the same answer can do that, using a simple question about colour.

What is a confederate?

A helper who secretly works with the researchers. In this study, two helpers were told to say green on purpose. The real participants didn't know this, so the researchers could see how they reacted.

Why were all the participants women?

All 172 participants in the 1969 study were women. That makes it harder to know whether the same thing would happen with men or with mixed groups.

What was the 1980 colour study about?

Moscovici and Personnaz (1980) tried to measure a more private kind of change. Each participant looked at blue slides, then at a white wall, and reported the colour of the afterimage they saw there. The authors reported that a minority could shift what people saw, not just what they said. Later researchers did not always get the same result, and many doubt the claim.

Is 8.42% the whole answer?

No. The clearest result is the difference between steady helpers and mixed helpers. Later tests did not all agree, so treat the numbers as one piece of evidence, not the final word.

What does this have to do with elections?

It is an analogy, not a conclusion. A group that holds its position steadily can make others reconsider their views. The study does not tell us which side in an election is right.
